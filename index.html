Case study: a system that had to last

Most game design gets evaluated in a playtest window. Long-form tabletop campaign design does not: the same players return every week for years, and every decision compounds. Consequence has to accumulate without the state space becoming unmanageable. Player agency has to be genuine, which means the authored structure must survive choices it did not anticipate. And the pacing has to hold across a horizon long enough that novelty stops doing any of the work.

The design method was deliberately operational: capture the state that mattered, turn observed behaviour into a design signal, change one mechanism at a time, and test the change in the next live session. The point was not to produce a clever isolated mechanic; it was to keep a whole engagement system coherent under real longitudinal pressure.

The retention metric has limits: it measures continued participation in a facilitated social experience, not an isolated A/B test. That limit is also why it is useful. It shows that the systems held up under the hardest condition design gets tested against: the same people, every week, for years, free to leave at any point.

Live-service economy and engagement design asks the same question this practice answered every week: what keeps someone coming back once novelty is gone and only the systems remain. The transferable method is simple and demanding — instrument behaviour, read the signal honestly, change one mechanism at a time, and judge it against retention rather than against how clever the design felt.
